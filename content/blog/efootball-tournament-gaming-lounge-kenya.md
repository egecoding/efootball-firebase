Walk into most gaming lounges in Nairobi on a weekend and you'll see the same thing: a queue of guys waiting for the PS5, a whiteboard with names scratched out and rewritten, and someone's phone open to a WhatsApp group arguing about who actually won the last match. It works, but it eats paid console time and it's the fastest way to send a customer home annoyed.

A bracket you run from your phone fixes most of that, and it costs nothing. This guide focuses on the big-console-lounge setup — if your crowd is more of a [WhatsApp group scene](/blog/efootball-tournaments-kenya-scene) than a walk-in venue, the same tool works there too.

## The real cost of running it on paper

Every minute spent figuring out "who plays who next" or settling a score dispute is a minute someone isn't paying to play. Multiply that by every round of a 16-player knockout and it adds up to real lost revenue on a busy Saturday — on top of the awkwardness of a lounge owner having to personally referee an argument between two customers.

## Set it up before your next tournament night

1. **[Create the tournament](/tournaments/new)** — pick a format (knockout is usually fastest for a lounge night), name it, and you're done. No install, works from your own phone browser.
2. **Share the invite link in your lounge's WhatsApp group.** This is the part that actually matters for a Kenyan lounge: your players don't need to download anything or make an account. They open the link, type their gamertag, and they're in. [Guest join](/blog/no-account-needed-guest-players) means the friction that normally kills sign-ups just isn't there.
3. **Players check their own bracket from their phone** while waiting for a console to free up, instead of hovering over the whiteboard.
4. **Scores get submitted with a screenshot**, and [AI scans the result screen automatically](/blog/ai-screenshot-scanning-and-fair-results) — so "he's lying about the score" stops being something you have to personally adjudicate between two customers.

## Why this fits how lounges actually operate

Consoles at a Kenyan gaming lounge are shared, rented by the hour, and rarely have anything installed on them beyond the games themselves — nobody's putting tournament software on a PS5 that ten different customers use in a day. That's exactly why this runs entirely from a browser, on whichever phone happens to be in someone's pocket. The organizer needs an account; nobody else does.

It also means you can run a proper tournament — with a real bracket, a shareable link, and a fair way to settle scores — for a one-off Saturday night event with zero setup cost and nothing to teach your regulars beyond "open this link."
