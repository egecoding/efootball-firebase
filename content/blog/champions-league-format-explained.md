If you've watched the modern UEFA Champions League, this format will look familiar — it's the same three-stage structure, adapted for an eFootball tournament of any size.

## Stage 1: The league phase

Instead of the old system of small, fixed groups where you only ever played the same three opponents, everyone plays a set number of rounds (the exact number scales with your tournament size) against **different** opponents each round — a Swiss-style pairing system, similar to chess tournaments. You're not stuck replaying the same rival four times; you face a spread of opponents across the whole field.

Every result earns points on a single combined table, same as a normal league: a win is worth more than a draw, goal difference breaks ties.

## Stage 2: The playoff round

Once the league phase wraps up, the table splits three ways:

- **Top finishers auto-qualify** straight into the knockout stage — no extra games, they've earned direct entry.
- **The mid-table band plays a knockout playoff round** — a two-legged tie to earn the remaining knockout spots.
- **Everyone below that is eliminated** — their tournament ends at the league phase.

This is what makes the format feel higher-stakes than a plain group stage: finishing 5th instead of 4th can mean an extra two matches you didn't need to play, and finishing in the bottom half means your tournament is over.

## Stage 3: The knockout stage

Whoever's left — the auto-qualifiers plus the playoff winners — goes into a standard single-elimination bracket from here on. One loss and you're out, all the way to the final.

## Why organizers pick this over a simpler format

The Champions League format solves a specific problem: a plain knockout gives short tournaments but no room for anyone to have one bad game and recover, while a plain league phase can drag on with no real knockout tension. This format gives you both — a long, meaningful league phase where good form actually counts across many games, and a genuine knockout finale once the fat's been trimmed.

The trade-off is length and complexity: it's the most involved format on the platform, both to explain to new players and to actually finish. It suits organizers running a longer competition — a season-style cup over a few weeks — rather than a single casual evening.
