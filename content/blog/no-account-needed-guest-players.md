The single biggest thing that kills a casual tournament before it starts is friction. If half your group has to create an account, verify an email, and remember a password just to play one match, half your group won't bother. So the platform is built around a simple rule: **only the organizer needs an account. Players don't.**

## How it actually works

When an organizer creates a tournament and shares the invite link, anyone who opens it can join by typing a nametag — that's it. No email, no password, no app download. Behind the scenes, that nametag is linked to a private participant identity stored in your browser, which is what lets you come back later, see your matches, and submit results, all without ever creating an account.

## What you can do as a guest

Once you've joined, you get your own player portal for that tournament. From there you can:

- See your upcoming match and who you're playing
- Submit your result, with an optional screenshot attached
- Get push notifications when your next match is ready (if you opt in)
- Dispute a result if something looks wrong
- Download a winner card if you take the whole thing

Basically everything a registered player can do, minus the ability to organize your own tournament (that part does need an account, since someone has to be accountable for the bracket).

## "I closed the tab — how do I get back in?"

This is the one real trade-off of guest access: your identity lives in your browser, not on a server tied to your email. If you switch devices or clear your browser data, you'll need to reclaim your spot by typing your exact nametag again on the tournament page — the system will match you back to your existing participant record by name.

If you know you'll want to switch devices mid-tournament, or you're playing in enough tournaments that you don't want to worry about this at all, creating a free account solves it permanently — your participation gets tied to your login instead of your browser.

## Why organizers should care about this

If you're the one setting up the tournament, this matters because it directly affects how many of the people you invite actually show up and play. A WhatsApp link that opens straight into "type your name and you're in" converts dramatically better than one that asks people to register first. It's the difference between a tournament with 16 real players and a tournament with 6 players and 10 people who meant to sign up but never got around to it.
